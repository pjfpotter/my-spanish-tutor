Memorise Ladder

The student starts with five signed-off answers they can't fully translate. They finish able to hear Tele ask a question in Spanish and say their answer with nothing on screen. Each answer climbs five rungs of help, in lessons of about two minutes.

What the student does

Memorizar.exe opens on five answers and one Start button. The app picks the lesson: a next-day check that is due comes first, then the answer already in progress, then the shortest answer not yet started. One answer goes all the way to the top before the next begins, so the first "I said it from memory" lands on day one.

Each answer is a short row of lessons.

  1. Meet it, about 9 steps. For every bit: Tele says it, the words and the English are on screen, the student says it back. Between bits there are "what does this mean?" taps, then tapping the answer into order. It ends with the whole answer after Tele, then read out alone. The student leaves knowing what every bit means and how it sounds.
  2. Build it, about 9 steps. A long answer gets one Build per sentence. The words shrink to first letters. Bit 1 alone, bit 2 alone, bits 1 and 2 together, bit 3 alone, bits 1 to 3, and so on, so each new bit is hooked onto what is already there. It ends with the whole answer from empty blocks.
  3. Wipe it, about 8 steps. The whole answer every time, and Tele asks the real question before each go. First letters, empty blocks, English only, then nothing but the question, twice, with a quick tap task in between so the second go is a real recall.
  4. Lock it in, 3 steps, opens the next day. Cold: Tele asks, nothing on screen, no warm-up. Three clean answers across two days and the answer is marked memorised.

Once two answers have reached the top, Shuffle appears: Tele asks those questions in a random order.

One step, tap by tap

  1. Tele speaks: the bit to copy, or the question.
  2. The student taps Speak, says it out loud, and taps Done.
  3. On a recall step the words come back so the answer can be checked.
  4. The panel at the bottom says what happened and names at most one bit to fix. A progress block fills. A clean step moves on by itself after a second.

How the ladder moves

  • A bit slips. The panel names that one bit. The next steps fix it: that bit alone one rung lower, then with the bit before it, then the same step again.
  • The same step slips twice. Tele puts words back. The rest of the lesson drops a rung, and the next lesson starts from where this one ended. Nothing is taken away and nothing is called failing.
  • Three clean first goes in a row. The rest of the lesson climbs a rung and repeated steps disappear, so the progress bar jumps.
  • Peek. Tap a hidden bit to flash it. The step still passes. It just isn't counted as clean.
  • "I know this one". Answer from the question alone before any lesson. Clean, and the answer skips straight to Lock it in.
  • The next-day check slips twice. The answer steps back to Wipe it.

Why it is built this way

  • Meaning before memory. Text that is understood is recalled better than text that is not, so every bit is met with its English before anything is hidden.
  • Say it out loud. Saying words aloud beats reading them silently (the production effect, MacLeod and colleagues 2010). Every core step is spoken. Tapping is only the breather.
  • Recall beats re-reading. Roediger and Karpicke 2006. Reading stops after the first lesson. From then on every step is a recall, with the check afterwards.
  • Fade the cues. Vanishing cues (Glisky, Schacter and Tulving 1986) and diminishing-cues retrieval practice (Fiechter and Benjamin 2018). These are the five rungs.
  • Build long texts bit on bit. The progressive-part method: 1, 2, 1 and 2, 3, 1 to 3. Long answers go one sentence at a time and are then joined.
  • Long texts break at the joins. "What comes next?" taps and the bit-with-its-neighbour fix train the links between bits.
  • Practise the way you are tested. Transfer-appropriate processing (Morris, Bransford and Franks 1977). The real question in Tele's voice starts every whole-answer go, on every rung.
  • Space it and sleep on it. Cepeda and colleagues 2006; successive relearning, Rawson and Dunlosky 2011. Tap tasks space the recalls inside a lesson, and Lock it in cannot open the same day.
  • Mix the questions. Shuffle makes the student tell the questions apart, which a single-answer lesson never asks of them.

The sources are cited from memory. Check them before quoting them to a school.

Stand-ins in this prototype

  • Marking. The student taps whether they said it all. The real app uses the recogniser. In Chrome on a computer, Tester tools can switch on real listening.
  • Voice. The five questions and two of Tele's reactions are the real Tele clips. The answer bits use the device's Spanish voice, because those clips are not built yet.
  • Days. "Skip to tomorrow" in Tester tools stands in for a night's sleep.

What this changes in the current spec

  • The last prompt is the Spanish question. The English meaning becomes rung 4, not the finish.
  • Memorise becomes lessons with a visible end, in place of an open practice loop.
  • The kinds of support are renamed and reordered: listen and repeat, first letters, empty blocks, English only, just the question.
